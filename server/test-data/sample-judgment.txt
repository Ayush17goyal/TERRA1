SUPREME COURT OF INDIA

Vishaka and Others v. State of Rajasthan
AIR 1997 SC 3011 | (1997) 6 SCC 241

DATE OF JUDGMENT: 13th August, 1997

BENCH: J.S. Verma (CJI), Sujata V. Manohar, B.N. Kirpal

FACTS

The petitioner, Vishaka, a non-governmental organization working for women's rights in Rajasthan, filed a Public Interest Litigation (PIL) under Article 32 of the Constitution of India. The immediate cause was the brutal gang rape of Bhanwari Devi, a social worker from a rural development programme of the Government of Rajasthan, who had attempted to prevent a child marriage in the community. The case highlighted the absence of any legislative framework to address sexual harassment of women in the workplace. The petitioner argued that such vacuum in law violated the fundamental rights guaranteed under Articles 14, 15, 19(1)(g) and 21 of the Constitution.

ISSUES FOR CONSIDERATION

1. Whether sexual harassment at the workplace constitutes a violation of fundamental rights under Articles 14, 15, 19(1)(g) and 21 of the Constitution of India.
2. Whether, in the absence of domestic legislation, international conventions and norms can be read into fundamental rights to fill the legislative vacuum.
3. What guidelines should be laid down by the Supreme Court to prevent sexual harassment at the workplace until appropriate legislation is enacted by Parliament.

ARGUMENTS OF THE PETITIONER

The petitioners submitted that the right to work with dignity is a universally recognized basic human right. They contended that sexual harassment of women at the workplace is incompatible with the dignity and honour of women and violates their fundamental right to equality and life with dignity. Reliance was placed on the Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination Against Women (CEDAW), to which India is a signatory, and on the Beijing Statement of Principles adopted at the Fourth World Conference on Women. The petitioners argued that in the absence of enacted law, the Court has the constitutional obligation to provide an effective remedy by laying down guidelines.

ARGUMENTS OF THE RESPONDENT

The State of Rajasthan did not seriously contest the need for safeguards against sexual harassment. The Union of India submitted that it was in the process of considering appropriate legislation on the subject but acknowledged the need for interim protective measures to fill the existing gap.

ANALYSIS

The Court observed that each incident of sexual harassment at the workplace results in violation of the fundamental rights of "Gender Equality" and the "Right to Life and Liberty." It is a clear violation of the rights under Articles 14, 15, 19(1)(g), and 21 of the Constitution. The Court drew upon international conventions and norms to interpret fundamental rights, noting that in the absence of domestic law occupying the field, any international convention not inconsistent with the fundamental rights and in harmony with its spirit must be read into these provisions to enlarge the meaning and content thereof. The Court relied specifically on Articles 11 and 24 of CEDAW and the General Recommendations of the CEDAW Committee.

The Court held that the content of fundamental rights guaranteed in the Constitution is of sufficient amplitude to encompass all the facets of gender equality including prevention of sexual harassment or abuse. The right to work in a safe environment free from sexual harassment is an integral part of Article 21. The Court also noted the duty of the employer to provide a safe working environment and the obligation of the State to ensure that rights are protected not only against State action but also against the acts of private individuals.

RATIO DECIDENDI

Sexual harassment at the workplace violates a woman's fundamental rights to equality, life, and liberty under Articles 14, 15, 19(1)(g) and 21 of the Constitution. In the absence of domestic legislation, international conventions and norms must be read into fundamental rights to fill the legislative vacuum. The Supreme Court, exercising its power under Article 32 read with Article 141, has the authority to lay down law in the form of guidelines to be treated as law declared under Article 141 until suitable legislation is enacted.

ORDER

The Supreme Court laid down detailed guidelines and norms to be strictly observed at all workplaces, public and private, until legislation is enacted. These guidelines, known as the "Vishaka Guidelines," included:

1. Definition of sexual harassment including unwelcome sexually determined behaviour such as physical contact, demand or request for sexual favours, sexually coloured remarks, showing pornography, and any other unwelcome physical, verbal or non-verbal conduct of a sexual nature.

2. Preventive steps to be taken by the employer including express prohibition of sexual harassment, publication of guidelines, and appropriate penalties.

3. Criminal proceedings under the IPC or any other law must be initiated by the employer when there is commission of a criminal offence.

4. A Complaints Committee headed by a woman with not less than half of its members being women must be established at every workplace.

5. Workers' initiative through awareness programmes and third-party intervention.

6. The guidelines to be treated as law declared by the Supreme Court under Article 141 of the Constitution.
